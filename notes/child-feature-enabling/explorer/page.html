Tree tensor networks on a Random Hierarchy Model · study of 25 Nov 2026

Does learning an easy root contrast speed up a hard one?

A tree tensor network learns to read the root symbol of a hierarchical grammar from its 16 leaves. Some functions of the root label are almost additive in the two symbols just below the root; others need the two children to interact. This study asks whether training on the easy kind builds the child features that the hard kind needs. In a planted two-level model the theory says yes, by a margin that grows without bound as the starting alignment shrinks. On three depth-four grammars, cutting only the easy target's gradient into the lower network slowed the hard target's half fit by about 2.4 times, and the cut network still learned it.

1 · The data

A grammar that writes 16 tokens from one root symbol

The Random Hierarchy Model (RHM) generates a sentence top-down. The root is one of 16 symbols. Every symbol has 4 productions, each an ordered pair of symbols one level down, chosen with probability 1/4. Four levels of binary productions give 16 observed tokens from a vocabulary of 16. At every level each pair of symbols appears in at most one production, so the leaves can be parsed back to the root without ambiguity. Two productions of the same symbol are synonyms: different child pairs that mean the same thing one level up.

Resampling draws new productions from the same rule table. Changing the grammar swaps the rule table and keeps the root and every production choice, so nearly every leaf changes while the choices stay the same.

2 · The learner

The network computes over the same tree, from the leaves up

The learner is a binary tree tensor network (TTN) laid over the same 16 positions. Each leaf token selects a 16-dimensional embedding. Each internal node fuses its two children's vectors with its own 16×16×16 core, za = Σbc Wabc zLb zRc, with no nonlinearity, and the root reads the outer product of the top two vectors. Above the shared leaf row is the grammar sampling top-down; below it is the network computing bottom-up on the same example. The exact parser inverts the rule table and returns symbols. The network never sees the rule table and returns vectors.

3 · The evidence

What was predicted, what was done, and what came out

The study has three parts that support different kinds of claim. The planted sweep checks a theorem numerically. The checkpoint readout observes classifiers that were trained earlier. The intervention is the causal test: two arms start from the same weights and see the same batches, and in one of them the easy head's error is stopped from reaching the lower cores while its head keeps training.

Acceleration, and why the detached arm's success matters

The data establish acceleration. The two arms differ only in one gradient path, share their start and their batches, and the intact arm reached half fit first in nine of nine pairs, 2.09 to 2.51 times sooner. That is a causal effect of the easy error's gradient on the lower cores, under plain SGD at this rate and initialization.

They do not establish necessity, and they argue against it. A stronger claim would be that the hard contrast cannot be learned without the feature the easy contrast teaches, as in the planted model, where the hard target alone takes about a thousand times longer at the smallest alignment. Here the detached arm, which sees nothing of the easy target, still reached half fit in every pair and went on to a fit of 0.9, from a representation exposing under a quarter of the easy contrast at half fit. On these grammars the easy gradient is a help, not a requirement.

The intact arm also simply sends more gradient into the lower cores (about 3.5 times the norm of the hard term's, nearly orthogonal to it), so whether it has to be this contrast is untested. A control with a second poorly additive contrast in place of the easy one would settle it.

How the protocol was amended

  1. Written protocol. Pilot rates 10⁻⁴, 3×10⁻⁴ and 10⁻³ for 300 updates on a separate pilot pair (grammar 0, init seed 9); the rule picked 10⁻³, with mean hard squared error 0.99996 (1 means no fit).
  2. Exploratory runs on the pilot pair only, rates 0.01 to 1 for 4,000 updates, showed the hard fit moving only at 0.1 and above. 4,000 updates at 10⁻³ is a gradient-flow time of 4; half fit later took 880 to 1,310.
  3. Amendment A3, before any evaluation pair ran at an amended rate: pilot rates 0.01 to 1, pilot length 4,000, horizon 40,000. Selection rule, pairs, starts, batches, loss and endpoint unchanged. The rule chose 0.3; rate 1 lost narrowly (mean final hard error 0.526 against 0.514) after its intact arm collapsed.
  4. A5, early stop. The first extended pair passed the endpoint by update 8,500 in both arms, so pairs now end once both arms have Dh ≥ 0.9, after both half-fit times are fixed. Everything was rerun from the new commit.
  5. A4, rate check. The three init-seed-0 pairs rerun at rate 0.1: ratios within 0.07 of rate 0.3.
  6. The written protocol is reported as it came out: all 18 arms censored. Five of its nine pairs had finished before any extended result existed; the saved files are from the rerun, which matched them.

Observational: classifiers trained earlier with cross-entropy and Adam

The nine original 16-way classifiers were saved at 15 points of training. Reading both contrasts out of their root features shows which becomes linearly available first. It cannot say what drove the lower cores.

4 · The theory

A planted grammar where the hard target needs a feature the easy one teaches

The planted model has four root classes encoded by two signs, (U, UV). The left child hides U and the right child hides V. The easy target U can be read from the left child alone; the hard target UV needs both. Everything below is exact: the 256 leaf strings are enumerated in your browser.

Rule table

Symbols are two bits, written 0–3 (first bit is the high bit). A bottom node over leaves x, x′ is x ⊕ x′. A root over children with first bits u, v is (u, u ⊕ v).

Why tying removes the second logarithmic wait

With the easy target present, the easy readout b and the left alignment rL feed each other linearly, so rL escapes from δ at rate λ1 = 1/φ ≈ 0.618, taking log(1/δ)/λ1.

When the two bottom nodes share one table, every update that rotates the left table rotates the right one too, so the right child arrives with the left and the hard fit follows in bounded time. In the saved sweep the wait from left half alignment to hard half fit is 1.0571 at every δ from 0.08 to 0.005.

With separate tables, the right alignment is still of order δ when the left one has learned. The right table only hears about V through the hard readout, and the pair (rR, w) is linearly unstable with rate λ2 = (√(φ²+4φ) − φ)/2 ≈ 0.698, so a second escape from order δ costs another log(1/δ)/λ2. The saved sweep's adjacent slopes are 1.4408, 1.4352, 1.4329 and 1.4321 against 1/λ2 = 1.4317.

Proved These are theorems about the planted model under population squared-loss gradient flow, as δ → 0. The planted sweep checks them numerically: at δ = 0.005 the hard half fit comes at 9.0 (tied) or 16.8 (untied) with the easy target, and at 10,013 or 20,016 without it.

Measured The depth-four numbers are not instances of these laws. They come from minibatch SGD at one initialization on three fixed grammars with 16 classes, where no contrast is purely interactive (the least additive one still has 6.3 to 10.8% of its variance explained additively). The factor 2.41 there has no δ to sweep and no proved prediction to match.

Derivation: population loss, gradients and the reduced flow

Write ai = E[hiHi], Ri = E[hi²] and ri = ai/√Ri, with HL = U, HR = V and expectations over the 16 equiprobable leaf pairs. The predictions are b hL for sU and w hLhR for UV. The two subtrees are independent, so the population loss is

2𝓛 = s² + 1 + b²RL + w²RLRR − 2sb aL − 2w aLaR.

Its derivatives give the untied parameter flow

ḃ = s aL − bRL,   ẇ = aLaR − wRLRR,
ḣL = (sb + w aR)HL − (b² + w²RR)hL,   ḣR = w aLHR − w²RLhR.

Norms are conserved quantities of the flow, RL = 1 + b² + w² and RR = 1 + w², and projecting the table velocities perpendicular to the tables gives

ṙL = (1 − rL²)(sb + w aR)/√RL,   ṙR = (1 − rR²) w aL/√RR.

The trained hard fit is Dh = rL²rR² − (w√(RLRR) − rLrR)², so its maximum over w is the ceiling rL²rR². In the tied model there is one table, both occurrences contribute to its gradient, and R = 1 + b² + 2w²; the reduced flow is ṙ = (1 − r²)(sb/√R + 2wr), ḃ = R(sr/√R − b), ẇ = R²(r²/R − w).

The second escape: once the left table has learned (rL → 1, b → 1/√φ, aL → √φ, RL → φ), the small pair z = (rR, w) obeys ż ≈ [[0, √φ], [√φ, −φ]] z, whose positive eigenvalue is λ2. With the easy target absent, the hard readout's gradient at w = 0 is −aLaR, of order δ², which is where the 1/δ² laws come from.

Full proofs, with the bounded-remainder arguments, are in the theory note (PDF).

Derivation: from the rule table to the joint law in the depth-four grammars

Put the conditional probability of each production into a three-index core, T(l)a,bc = (1/m)·[(b, c) is a production of a]. Contracting the cores upward with one-hot leaf vectors gives the likelihood of the leaves under each root symbol; multiplying by the uniform root prior gives the joint law P(x, y). For a legal string in the depth-four grammars it is (1/16)(1/4)15 = 2−34, one root and 15 production choices, and the posterior over the root is one-hot. Two student copies observing the same leaves give the expected squared output, so the population squared loss of any aligned tree network, and its gradient for every core, can be contracted exactly without sampling. That is how the ceilings A and fits D in section 3 are computed.

The contrasts come from the 64 top productions alone. With E encoding the root label, X the two child symbols as concatenated one-hots and PX the projector onto additive functions of them, the eigenvectors of the label-space operator Q⊤PXQ with the largest and smallest eigenvalue are the easy and hard contrasts, and the eigenvalues are their additive fractions.
